Reported Speech

Statements & Questions — Shifting from Present to Past Tense

📖

Introduction

When we report what someone said, we use reported speech (indirect speech). We do not always repeat the exact words — the verb tense shifts one step back into the past.

🗣️ Direct: "I am hungry."

📝 Reported: She said (that) she was hungry.

  • Use said or told + object to introduce the report.
  • The word that is optional but very common.
  • Pronouns usually change to match the new speaker's point of view.
📢

Reported Statements

A statement is a declarative sentence (not a question). To report it:

  • Use said (no object) or told + someone
  • Optionally add that
  • Shift the verb tense one step back into the past
  • Change pronouns as needed (e.g. I → she, my → her)
Pattern: Subject + said / told (sb) + (that) + subject + past verb…
Direct
"I love music."
Reported
She said (that) she loved music.
Direct
"We are studying English."
Reported
They said they were studying English.
Direct
"Tom has a new car."
Reported
She told me Tom had a new car.
❓

Reported Questions

Reported questions use asked, follow normal statement word order (subject before verb, no inversion), and remove the auxiliary do / does / did.

Yes/No questions: Subject + asked + if / whether + subject + past verb

Wh- questions: Subject + asked + wh-word + subject + past verb

Yes/No Direct
"Do you have a pen?"
Reported
She asked if I had a pen.
Wh- Direct
"Where do you live?"
Reported
He asked me where I lived.
Be Direct
"Are you tired?"
Reported
She asked if I was tired.
Wh- Direct
"What can you do?"
Reported
He asked what I could do.

📄 The School Interview

Yesterday, a journalist visited Lincoln School and interviewed two students about their school life. The first student, Lucia, said that she loved her English class because the teacher was very creative. She also mentioned that she was preparing a project about endangered animals for the science fair.

The second student, Marco, told the journalist that he could play the guitar and that he would perform at the school concert the following month. When the journalist asked if he was nervous, Marco replied that he was a little nervous but very excited. He added that his classmates were very supportive and kind.

At the end of the interview, Lucia asked the journalist what his favourite subject had been at school. The journalist smiled and said it was Science.

Comparative Adverbs

Comparing Actions — How We Do Things Differently

📖

Introduction

Adverbs describe how, when, where, or how much an action is done. Comparative adverbs compare the way two people or things perform an action.

🏃 Simple: Maria runs fast.

⚡ Comparative: Maria runs faster than Tom.

📐 Comparative: Tom speaks more carefully than Maria.

  • We use than to introduce the second thing being compared.
  • Short adverbs (1 syllable) usually add -er.
  • Long adverbs (2+ syllables, ending in -ly) use more + adverb.
  • Some adverbs are irregular (well → better, badly → worse).
📏

Formation Rules

Rule 1 — Short adverbs: add -er (and -er than in a sentence)

fast → faster  |  hard → harder  |  late → later  |  high → higher

Rule 2 — Long adverbs (-ly): use more + adverb

carefully → more carefully  |  quickly → more quickly

slowly → more slowly  |  quietly → more quietly

Rule 3 — Irregular adverbs: completely different forms

well → better  |  badly → worse  |  far → further / farther  |  little → less

Simple Adverb
She sings beautifully.
Comparative
She sings more beautifully than her sister.
Simple Adverb
He works hard.
Comparative
He works harder than his brother.
Simple Adverb
She speaks well.
Comparative
She speaks better than me.

📄 The School Sports Day

Last Friday, Lincoln School held its annual Sports Day. Many students participated in different competitions. In the 100-metre race, Carlos ran faster than all the other students and won first place. His friend Diego ran quickly too, but Carlos ran more quickly than Diego.

In the swimming competition, Sofia performed better than last year because she had been training harder. Her teammate Lucy swam well, but Sofia swam more confidently than anyone else in the pool.

The long jump was very exciting. Pedro jumped further than his rivals, but he also landed less gracefully than expected and nearly fell over. Everyone laughed kindly. The teachers said the students had all worked harder than in previous years, and the results showed it clearly.

✅ She sings more loudly than her friend. — "loudly" is the correct adverb form.
